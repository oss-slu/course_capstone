This document explains how sprints work in Open Source with SLU and what's expected of you as a developer during each sprint cycle.

## Why Sprints?

Software development in the real world rarely happens in one big push. Professional teams break work into smaller, predictable cycles—delivering working software frequently rather than waiting until "everything is done."

Sprints are how we practice this. Each sprint is a bounded commitment: you pick up a piece of work, you deliver it, and then you reflect on what happened. This rhythm creates:

- **Accountability**: You're delivering something concrete every two weeks, not just "working on stuff"
- **Feedback loops**: Regular check-ins catch problems early, when they're easier to fix
- **Professional habits**: The ability to estimate, communicate progress, and ship on a schedule is a career skill

The sprint structure isn't arbitrary bureaucracy—it mirrors how software teams at companies large and small actually operate.

## The Two-Week Sprint Cycle

Each sprint runs for two weeks, Monday to Monday. The specific dates for each sprint are published in Canvas under the Sprint Structure & Schedule module.

Here's what a typical sprint looks like:

### Week 1: Build

**Monday (Sprint Start)**
Your tech lead has prepared issues for the sprint. By 6:00 PM Monday, there should be a pool of work ready for your team to pull from. If you're unsure what to work on, ask in your project's Slack channel.

**Tuesday through Sunday**
This is heads-down development time. You should be:

- Working through your assigned issue(s)
- Asking questions early when you're stuck (Slack, office hours, teammates)
- Pushing commits regularly—not waiting until the last minute
- Opening a draft PR as soon as you have something reviewable

**Monday (End of Week 1)**
By 4:10 PM, you need to have raised a functional pull request. "Functional" means:

- The code runs without breaking existing functionality
- It addresses the issue you were assigned (even if incomplete)
- It's ready for your tech lead to review

This mid-sprint deadline exists because code review takes time. Your tech lead needs to see your work while there's still a week to iterate.

### Week 2: Refine and Merge

**Tuesday through Friday**
Your tech lead will review your PR and likely request changes. This is normal. Professional code rarely merges on the first try.

During this phase:

- Respond to review feedback promptly (within 24 hours)
- Ask clarifying questions if you don't understand the feedback
- Push fixes and request re-review
- Communicate in the PR thread, not just Slack—documentation matters

**Monday (Sprint End)**
By 4:10 PM, your PR should be fully merged. This is a firm deadline.

Your tech lead will either merge your work or close the PR. They won't merge broken code just because the sprint is ending—the work has to be worth merging.

## What's Expected of You

### The Baseline: One Merged PR Per Sprint

At minimum, you are expected to complete **one full PR cycle per sprint**—from Issue assignment through merged code. This means:

1. Taking ownership of an Issue
2. Raising a functional PR by Week 1 deadline
3. Responding to review feedback
4. Getting your PR merged by the sprint end

Consistently delivering one merged PR per sprint demonstrates that you can participate in a professional development workflow. However, **baseline participation reflects minimal engagement** and corresponds to C-level performance in the course.

### Beyond Baseline: What Distinguishes Stronger Performance

One PR per sprint is the floor, not the ceiling. Stronger contributions include:

**Progress toward Milestones**
Your team has iteration Milestones—larger goals you're working toward across multiple sprints. Contributing work that meaningfully advances these Milestones demonstrates that you understand the bigger picture, not just individual tickets.

**Overall product value**
Some contributions matter more than others. Fixing a critical bug, implementing a key feature for a client demo, or unblocking a teammate's work creates outsized value. Look for high-impact opportunities, not just easy wins.

**Multiple PRs when appropriate**
If your assigned Issue is smaller or you complete it early, pick up additional work. Sustained momentum across sprints—not occasional heroics—builds trust and demonstrates professional reliability.

**Quality and craftsmanship**
Well-documented code, thoughtful commit messages, proactive testing, and clean PR descriptions show that you care about your work and respect your teammates' time.

### The Path to Excellence

An A in this course requires completing an Excellence assignment—work that goes significantly beyond baseline expectations. Excellence activities include scholarly contributions, technical innovation, community building, or leadership within your project.

Plan for Excellence early. Discuss ideas with your tech lead and the instructor so your Excellence work aligns with both your interests and project goals.

## What Gets Submitted

At the end of each sprint, you submit a **Developer Contribution Report** through Canvas. This includes:

1. **Demo video** (2–3 minutes): Show your working feature as if presenting to a client
2. **Written report**: PR links, a sprint reflection (~500–750 words), and optionally a work log

The specific submission requirements are detailed in each Sprint Close assignment. The reflection asks you to evaluate your delivery, collaboration, technical growth, and professional development—this is for your benefit, not just a grading checkbox.

## When Things Don't Go as Planned

Not every sprint goes smoothly. Here's how to handle common situations:

### "My issue is bigger than I thought"

Talk to your tech lead early—ideally by Wednesday of Week 2. They can help rescope the work:

- The current issue gets reduced to something achievable
- The removed work moves to a new issue for a future sprint
- You still deliver something functional, even if smaller than planned

Rescoping is a professional skill, not a failure. Identifying scope creep early is valuable.

### "I'm stuck and can't make progress"

Raise this immediately, not at the sprint deadline. Resources available to you:

- Your tech lead (that's literally their job)
- Teammates who might have solved similar problems
- Slack channels (your project channel, #tech-help)
- Office hours

Suffering in silence for two weeks helps no one.

### "I ran out of time"

If your PR isn't merged by Monday and the issue was reasonably scoped, your tech lead may give you until Wednesday to finish (with reduced credit). But this is a grace period, not an expectation.

The sprint cycle is designed to be achievable. If you're consistently running out of time, that's a signal to discuss with your tech lead—either about scope, time management, or both.

### "My PR got closed without merging"

This happens when the work doesn't meet the bar for merging—perhaps it breaks existing functionality, doesn't actually solve the issue, or introduces more problems than it solves.

This isn't punishment; it's how professional development works. Understand what went wrong, learn from it, and do better next sprint.
